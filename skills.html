What I can do, and how I know

Skills

01

Technical proficiency

I spent the internship working in a production codebase I did not write, across the frontend, testing, and documentation. I shipped over 100 bug fixes and features, working in Claude Code with tasks tracked in Linear. I also reviewed other interns' pull requests, which meant reading code critically rather than only producing it.

The clearest test was not a scheduled task. At our user workshop, an attendee could not connect his LinkedIn account due to an issue I had yet to encounter. I did not have an answer, so I diagnosed it live during the session and got back to him before it ended. What I took from that is that not knowing is a normal state, and the useful skill is being fast and honest about closing the gap rather than appearing to know.

Adaptability

Our Product Hunt launch was a single-day event with a hard deadline and no ability to push it. Because Product Hunt ranks on US Pacific time, our Singapore-based day ran from noon to eleven at night. There was no useful distinction between whose job something was. If a comment needed a reply or an asset needed redoing, whoever was free did it. I monitored support channels and reached out to get eyes on the listing, and I stopped waiting to be assigned things.

It was also the first time my work was public and could be wrong in front of people, which is a different pressure than a pull request a teammate catches before anyone sees it. The hard work paid off: we finished the week as Product of the Week.

Communication soft skill

At the user workshop I was live floor support for about thirty working professionals, most of them older than me, all working in English as a shared professional language rather than a first one for everyone. Very few said outright that they were lost. I had to read a stalled screen or a hesitation instead of waiting for a raised hand, and I stopped treating silence as agreement. When someone goes quiet now, I assume I have lost them rather than that they are following.

The instinct I had to correct was reaching for someone's laptop and doing it for them. That solves the immediate problem and teaches nothing. Talking someone through it is slower and it is the actual job. The same standard applied to my written communication with a team spread across Singapore, India, and Australia, where an incomplete message cost a full day of waiting for the answer. It also changed how I treat my own explanations: if I have to talk a user through a step, the step is the problem, and that goes back to the team rather than staying in my head.
